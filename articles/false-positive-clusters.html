False-positive clusters in citations and template lines ≠ whole-file AI writing

Charles Li / HumanPen

Open an AI writing report and you may see highlights stacked on the literature review: citation strings, “this study aims to…”, methods boilerplate, and transition formulas. Group chats then treat the map as “the whole file is AI.” That leap is usually wrong. A false-positive cluster means the estimator reacted to a dense pocket of formulaic continuous prose—not that every paragraph is machine-authored.

This note is detector-report literacy for international students. It does not promise any score, and it is not a detection-evasion guide.

Why citations and templates light up together

Reference-heavy sentences, reused assignment stems, and discipline templates share surface statistics with a lot of training text. Tools that score continuous prose can paint a bright band across that pocket while your results discussion stays pale or blank. Students in India and Southeast Asia often paste the methods template their lecturer shared—then panic when only that block is flagged.

The percentage still summarizes scored continuous prose. A cluster in the citation zone does not automatically infect the conclusion.

How to read a cluster without rewriting everything

  1. Map the pocket: list which pages/paragraphs are dense with highlights vs which are clean.
  2. Separate instruments: similarity (matched sources) vs AI writing (model-like style). A citation cluster can trigger either or both—state them separately in office hours.
  3. Keep verified citations frozen unless the source is wrong. Do not “paraphrase away” every reference string to chase a quieter map.
  4. Edit only the flagged continuous prose you actually authored—clarify your wording, methods narrative, and claims—then re-export the same attempt identity.

What a cluster is not

A cluster is not a whole-file authorship verdict. It is not proof of plagiarism by itself. It is not a reason to delete the bibliography. Incomplete processing or pale confidence bands can make clusters look scarier than the footnotes allow.
