Mark page or paragraph numbers next to flagged spans before the meeting

Charles Li / HumanPen

Office hours stall when both people scroll hunting for “the middle paragraph.” Before you go, label each flagged span with a page or paragraph number on the printout or PDF markup. Then you can point once and talk about the brief, not the scrollbar.

This is distinct from flagged-span-checklist, from triage-flagged-sentences, from one-question-per-meeting, from legend-before-rewrite, from crop-hides-attempt-id, and from save-export-with-attempt-id. Here the literacy move is concrete: page/paragraph numbers on the span before you walk in.

This note is detector-report literacy. It does not promise any score, and it is not a detection-evasion guide.

Better order

  1. Open the named full export for that attempt ID.
  2. List the spans you will discuss; write p.# / ¶# (or line range) beside each on paper or sticky notes in the PDF.
  3. Bring the numbered print/markup + matching submission file.
  4. In the meeting, say “page 4, paragraph 2” first—then the question.
  5. Copy those numbers into your post-meeting takeaways.

Why numbers beat hand-waving

Tutors in India and Southeast Asia English-medium programs share a short slot and often a shared screen. Vague location talk burns minutes. Fixed page/paragraph anchors keep one-question meetings on content, not navigation.

What this is not

  • Not a guarantee the next badge will clear.
  • Not advice to debate every highlight—number the ones you actually ask about.
  • Not a substitute for a formal integrity process if one is opened.

Where HumanPen fits

HumanPen at humanpen.net supports document-level revision against a report once you know which numbered spans to touch. No guaranteed next percentage.

Remember

  • Page/paragraph numbers beside flagged spans.
  • “Middle bit” wastes the meeting.
  • Point once, then ask the question.
  • Honest tools do not promise a clean next card.
