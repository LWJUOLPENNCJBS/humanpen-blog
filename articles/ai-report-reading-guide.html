How to Read an AI Writing Report: A Complete Guide from Number to Decision

Charles Li / HumanPen

The message usually arrives at the worst possible moment: the day before a deadline, right after a supervision meeting, or as a screenshot forwarded in the class group chat. On screen there is one large number — a percentage, a badge, a coloured band — and behind it sit dozens of pages nobody has read.

The problem was never the report. It is that we turn one number into a verdict about an entire document. This guide collects in one place what is usually scattered across short notes: what the report measures and what it does not, the three numbers that cannot be compared, how to move from the report to a decision with a bounded scope, what must stay intact after any revision, and what to bring to office hours. It is about reading and procedure, not a promise of outcome.

1. What the report measures, and what it does not

AI writing detectors are not truth machines. They are statistical classifiers that assign a score to each passage based on surface features: how regular the sentence lengths are, how repetitive the paragraph structure is, the absence of stylistic irregularity, the distribution of punctuation, and sometimes vocabulary patterns. The result should be read as a probability, not as evidence.

That is precisely why a text written entirely by a person can come back flagged, and a text generated entirely by a machine can pass unflagged. Neither case is a rare malfunction; both are the direct consequence of how the tool works. Which gives the first rule of this guide: a report is a probability map, not a verdict.

What the report does not measure, and what is worth saying to yourself before you argue about anything:

  • It does not measure whether the idea is original, whether the argument holds, or whether the sources are reliable.
  • It does not measure your effort, your hours, or how many drafts you deleted.
  • It cannot reliably compare against other texts once the language, version, or coverage changes.
  • It does not produce a "plagiarism percentage" — similarity and stylistic features are two entirely separate systems.

2. The three numbers that cannot be compared

The most common error I see in class group chats: putting two figures side by side as if they came from the same ruler. In fact three numbers of different natures coexist on a single report, and none of them converts into another by any arithmetic operation:

NumberWhat it meansWhy it cannot be compared
Reported percentageAn estimate of the generated share of the documentDepends on the detector version and the range examined
CoverageHow much of the document actually entered the checkA 60% coverage file is not comparable to a 95% one
Detector versionThe model version that issued the judgementA version change invalidates comparison across time

If you read "45%" on a report with 62% coverage and an older version, then "18%" on a report with 97% coverage and a newer version, you are not seeing an improvement — you are seeing the instrument change. The operational rule: compare two numbers only when coverage, version, and the examined range all match. When they do not, that is not a comparison, it is an impression.

3. Asterisks, dashes, and confidence bands

Many reports do not give a single number but a range: an asterisk, a dash, or a bar running from grey to red. The range is not decoration — it is an explicit admission of uncertainty, and it should be read as an admission rather than compressed away. In short:

  • Asterisk usually means "below the display threshold", not "zero". Zero and "below threshold" are not the same thing.
  • Dash can mean "not examined" or "still processing". Wait for processing to finish before deciding.
  • Pale band indicates low confidence in that passage. Treat it as neither absolution nor conviction.

The second common error: treating the pale zone as a "safe zone", rewriting everything around it and leaving it alone. The correct move is often the opposite — high-confidence passages are the ones to raise with your instructor first, because they are the most stable across versions.

4. From report to decision: five steps

Do not start by revising. Start by fixing. The order below prevents both rework and unresolvable argument:

  1. Fix the examined version. Keep the original file exactly as it is, with the date and the attempt ID in the filename. Every later revision must be compared against that file, not against your memory of it.
  2. Download the full report. A screenshot of the badge is not a report. You need the PDF or the full report page, with coverage, version, and date.
  3. Build a passage table. Three columns: passage number, confidence level, and whether the course instruction requires a revision. Do not write anything else at this stage.
  4. Ask one concrete question. Not "is this good?" but "passage three is flagged at high confidence — am I asked to revise it, or to discuss it in the report?"
  5. Set the scope before opening the editor. Write one line: "I am revising passages 3 and 7 only." Anything outside that line stays as it is.

5. Revision scope: only the passages that were marked

The most common reaction — "I'll rewrite the whole document to be safe" — is usually the worst option available. Not only for ethical reasons, but for a purely procedural one: rewriting everything makes you lose what was already right. Sentences that were precise turn vague, references that were tied to their numbers come loose, the table that matched the text starts contradicting it.

Bounded revision means: defined input, defined output, and a verifiable effect. Revise one paragraph and you can show what changed. Rewrite the whole document and you can prove nothing — not to your instructor, not to yourself. The rule: if you cannot describe the revision in one sentence beginning "I revised…", you did not revise: you rewrote. And rewriting is not revision; it is a new document carrying the old name.

6. What must stay intact

After any revision, these elements must remain exactly as they were. If one changes, the problem is not stylistic but technical, and it is resolved before anything else:

  • References and citation numbering. Renumbering the bibliography breaks in-text citations; it is the first thing an instructor notices.
  • Tables and their captions. A missing row, or a caption separated from its table, means the file is not submittable.
  • Footnotes and endnotes. They must stay tied to their numbers in the body text.
  • Equations and symbols. Turning an equation into an image or plain text destroys its meaning.
  • Styles and heading levels. A heading that loses its level breaks the table of contents automatically.
  • Page layout. Margins, page breaks, columns — these are part of the document, not ornament.

This list is also a test list: open the file after revising and compare it with the original item by item. It takes two minutes and catches most disasters before submission.

7. Formats: DOCX, PDF, and PPTX

The report may concern a Word document, an exported PDF, or a presentation. Each format behaves differently when you revise:

  • DOCX: styles, references, and footnotes survive relatively well, but moving the file between different tools loses conditional formatting.
  • PDF: not an editing format. Revising it means rebuilding, or converting to DOCX first — and a careless conversion produces text with no structure.
  • PPTX: the most fragile. Slide text is bound to fixed-size text boxes; any increase in length pushes text out of the box or auto-shrinks the font. The fix is not "write less" but revising inside the box without rebuilding the slide.

The right question before any tool: "will it give the file back in the same format and the same structure?" If the answer is unclear, you are about to create extra work, not a revision.

8. Office hours: what to bring and what to ask

Office hours are not a courtroom and should not turn into a defence. They work best with three things: the examined version, the full report, and one concrete question. What you do not bring matters too: not five drafts, not an explanation of "how AI works" — that is not the topic of the conversation.

The three questions that in practice close almost every discussion:

  1. "Am I asked to revise the flagged passages, or to discuss the report in a memo?"
  2. "If coverage is not complete, is the result considered sufficient for a decision?"
  3. "What would you like to see in the next version: the revised passages, or a record of what I changed?"

9. Why the error margin grows outside English

Most detectors were trained and tuned primarily on English text. When the model is applied to Chinese, Arabic, Turkish, Indonesian, or Vietnamese, the accuracy of surface features drops: punctuation conventions differ, sentence structure differs, and in languages without word spacing, segmentation and function-word linkage produce variation that has nothing to do with style.

The practical consequence: in non-English text the percentage is less stable across versions, and the boundary between "flagged" and "unflagged" is blurrier. That does not mean the report is worthless; it means the error band is wider, and asking your instructor matters more than asking the number.

This is why HumanPen works in more than thirty languages rather than English alone: a revision that preserves the structural features of a Chinese or Arabic text is not a translated version of an English revision. Full-width versus half-width punctuation, spacing in mixed-script text, terminology consistency — these are part of the document, not details fixed afterwards.

There is a second layer for many students: thinking in one language and submitting in English. The two error sources then stack — the English text has its own stylistic drift, and your writing habits carry L1 syntax (stacked modifiers, subjectless fragments, connective density). Part of what the detector reads as "irregularity" is language transfer, not an AI trace. That is worth stating directly to your instructor; it is more useful than a defence.

10. Common mistakes that consume time

  • Judging the document from the badge. The badge summarises one page; the full report carries coverage and version, and both change what the number means.
  • Comparing two reports with different coverage. Not an improvement, a different measurement. See section 2.
  • Rewriting everything "to be safe". Loses what was right and proves nothing. See section 5.
  • Revising while sharing your screen. Nobody needs to watch your draft change during the meeting.
  • Citing numbers from memory. If the number is not in the file in front of you, do not use it in a discussion.
  • Confusing similarity with stylistic features. Two systems; mixing them derails the conversation from the first sentence.

12. The limits of this material

This material does not guarantee passing any detector, does not explain how to hide generated text, and does not offer "tricks" for getting around checking systems. Its aim is different: to understand what the report actually says, to act within a bounded scope you can defend, and to keep the document intact while doing so. If your course prohibits the use of rewriting tools, the problem is not the report — it is the policy, and that is resolved with your instructor, not with a tool.

Summary in six points

  1. A report is a probability map, not a verdict about your text.
  2. Do not compare two numbers until coverage, version, and examined range match.
  3. Asterisk, dash, and pale band are admissions of uncertainty at different degrees — read them that way.
  4. Fix the examined version first, then read, then set the scope, then revise.
  5. Revise only the flagged passages; a full rewrite takes the correct parts with it.
  6. After revising, check in order: references, tables, footnotes, equations, heading levels, and page layout.
